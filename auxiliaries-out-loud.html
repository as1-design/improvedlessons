Pronunciation · B2 · auxiliary verbs

Three ways to say
the same two words.

In English, a lot of the meaning doesn't sit in the words themselves. It sits in which syllable you say strongly and which way your voice moves at the end. Listen to these three. The words are identical every time.

Interested
Are you?

The auxiliary is strong and the voice goes up. This one means go on, tell me more.

Bored
Are you.flat

The same words, but the voice stays flat. It sounds as though you'd rather be somewhere else.

Disbelief
ARE you?

A very strong auxiliary and the voice drops. Now it means I don't believe you.

Overview

What we're going to practise

It's the same small word doing the work each time — do, have, are, can. What changes is how strongly you say it, and whether the subject gets a strong beat as well.

1
Reply questions — Are you?
The auxiliary is stressed, your voice goes up, and you sound interested.
2
Contrast — I have.
Both the subject and the auxiliary are stressed, and your voice falls.
3
So do I / Neither do I
The auxiliary goes quiet and the two words around it take the stress.
4
Question tags — isn't it?
The auxiliary and the subject share the stress, and the tune tells us what you mean.
5
Emphasis — I do like it!
The auxiliary is said very strongly, because you're insisting on something.
+
And underneath it all, rhythm
The strong beats stay evenly spaced, and the auxiliaries get squeezed in between.
Pattern 1

Reply questions

↗
Stress the auxiliary and let your voice go up, just as you would in a real question. You're not actually asking for information here — you're letting the other person know that you're listening.
A
I'm a vegetarian.
B
Are you?
A
My sister works for Google.
B
Does she?
A
I don't eat before a class.
B
Don't you?
A
We've been living here for ten years.
B
Have you?

One thing to watch: if your voice stays flat here, it doesn't sound neutral in English. It can come across as sarcastic, or as though you aren't interested. All the warmth is in the rise, so don't worry about overdoing it.

Pattern 2

Saying that you're different

↘
When you want to say that you — or someone else — are different, stress both the subject and the auxiliary and let your voice fall. You end up with two strong beats next to each other.
A
I've never been to a fortune teller.
B
I have.
A
Nobody liked the film.
B
I did.
A
None of us can drive.
B
She can.
A
You're not even listening.
B
I am.
Notice that you can't contract these. I'm is fine inside a longer sentence, but on its own you need the full form, because the auxiliary is the word carrying the stress: I am. · She is. · We have. · They were.
Pattern 3

So do I · Neither do I

✱
This time the auxiliary is unstressed and said very quickly. The strong beats land on the words on either side of it — So and I — and the auxiliary gets squeezed into the middle.
A
I believe in ghosts.
B
So do I.
/ˌsəʊ də ˈaɪ/ — do reduces to /də/
A
I can't swim.
B
Neither can I.
/ˈnaɪðə(r) kən ˈaɪ/ — can reduces to /kən/
A
I was born in Tarragona.
B
So was I.
/ˌsəʊ wəz ˈaɪ/
A
I've never tried sushi.
B
Neither have I.
/ˈnaɪðə(r) həv ˈaɪ/ — have reduces to /həv/ or /əv/

Two things to watch: the auxiliary comes before the subject — Neither I can becomes Neither can I. And neither is already negative, so the verb stays positive: not Neither can't I.

A closer look

Getting neither right

1 · The vowel is up to you

Neither has two pronunciations, /ˈnaɪðə(r)/ and /ˈniːðə(r)/, and you'll hear both on both sides of the Atlantic. UK/ˈnaɪ-/ is the more common British one; US/ˈniː-/ the more common American one. Plenty of speakers do the opposite, though, and neither version is wrong. Either works in exactly the same way.

If someone asks which one to use: choose whichever feels comfortable and stay with it.

2 · The difference that is reliable: the r

USAmerican accents are rhotic, so the r is always pronounced: /ˈniːðər/.

UKStandard British is non-rhotic, so the r is silent: /ˈnaɪðə/. It comes back, though, when a vowel follows, as a linking /r/: neither of us /ˈnaɪðər əv ˈʌs/.

This difference really is reliable, which is why it's the one worth teaching. The vowel isn't.

3 · Me neither and Me either

Me neither. Informal, but perfectly standard in both varieties, so it's a safe one to use anywhere.

Me either. USAmerican and informal only. It isn't used in British English, and a good many American speakers don't accept it either.

Nor do I. A slightly more formal way of saying Neither do I, used in both varieties.

4 · None of this changes the rhythm

NEIther can I.

Whichever vowel you go for, the stress stays on the first syllable and the auxiliary in the middle stays weak. The pronunciation question is interesting, but it's this strong–weak–strong shape that actually makes you sound natural.

Pattern 4

Question tags, and what your voice does

⇅
The auxiliary and the subject in the tag get about the same amount of stress. What your voice does next is what tells us whether you're really asking something or just making conversation.
voice goes up ↗ you really are asking
You haven't seen my car keys, have you?

I honestly don't know whether you've seen them, and I'd like an answer.

voice goes down ↘ you expect them to agree
It's a beautiful day, isn't it?

I already know you'll agree with me. This is small talk rather than a question, so it comes out sounding like a statement.

Up or down? Let's decide together

You couldn't lend me five euros, could you?

You're asking a favour, so you don't know what they'll say.

That exam was impossible, wasn't it?

You both sat it, so you already know they agree.

You don't know where Anna is, do you?

You really are trying to find her.

It's freezing in here, isn't it?

You can both feel it. This is conversation, not a question.

Vote with your hands first — does the voice go up or down?
Pattern 5

When you really mean it

!
You can add do, does or did to a positive sentence that wouldn't normally need an auxiliary, and then say it very strongly. It means something like I know you don't believe me, but it's true.

Someone thinks you didn't like it.

I do like your new haircut!

Someone says you never called.

I did phone you — twice.

If there's already an auxiliary, just stress that one.

You have grown!

Agreeing with someone, up to a point.

She does work hard, to be fair.

A grammar point worth noting: after emphatic do / does / did, the main verb goes back to its base form — I did phone you, not I did phoned you.

Rhythm

Why the beat stays the same

English is stress-timed, which means the strong beats come at roughly equal intervals. As we add auxiliaries the sentence gets longer, but the number of beats doesn't change. The auxiliaries just get squeezed in between.

A few UK / US differences worth noting down
UKHave you got a car? → Yes, I have.  ·  USDo you have a car? → Yes, I do. The auxiliary in your short answer matches the one in the question.
UKneither most often /ˈnaɪðə/, no final r unless a vowel follows  ·  USmost often /ˈniːðər/, r always sounded. Both vowels occur in both varieties. Me neither. works everywhere; Me either. is American and informal only.
UKI've just eaten. → Have you?  ·  USI just ate. → Did you? The tense you choose changes the auxiliary you reply with.
★
If you remember one thing: the auxiliary is never just a grammar word. Stressed with a rising voice, it shows interest. Stressed with a falling voice, it disagrees. Unstressed, it agrees. Once you know where the beat goes, you sound like you mean what you're saying.
